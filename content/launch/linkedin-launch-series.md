## Post 1 — The problem (why change initiatives fail)

**Hook:** Most change programs don't fail on the strategy. They fail on the behavior.

I've spent my career as a psychologist inside organizations that were trying to change
something: how people sell, how they lead, how citizens act on a new policy. The pattern is
almost always the same.

Leaders assume that once people understand the change, they'll adopt it. They won't.
Understanding and behavior are different systems. You can explain the "why" perfectly and watch
nothing move.

So the plan gets more communication. More town halls. More slides. And the behavior still
doesn't budge, because the actual barrier was never about understanding. It was about capability,
or opportunity, or motivation, and nobody diagnosed which.

The teams that succeed treat change the way a psychologist treats behavior change. They find out
what's really holding the current behavior in place before they push a new one.

That diagnosis is the step almost everyone skips.

## Post 3 — Methodology explainer (CLEAR / COM-B, concrete)

**Hook:** "We need people to change" is not a plan. Here's how to turn it into one.

When someone isn't doing a behavior you want, there are only three reasons. Behavioral science
calls this COM-B:

- **Capability** — they can't. They lack the skill, the knowledge, or the mental bandwidth.
- **Opportunity** — the environment won't let them. It's inconvenient, invisible, or socially
  discouraged.
- **Motivation** — they don't want to, or something else wins in the moment.

Almost every failed change effort is a mismatch: the barrier is Opportunity, but the intervention
was aimed at Motivation. You ran a campaign to inspire people when the real problem was that the
right action took eleven clicks.

Concrete example: [ERIK: insert a real, non-confidential example — e.g. a case where a behavior
looked like a motivation problem but was actually an opportunity/friction problem].

CLEAR forces the diagnosis before the solution. Clarify the behavior, map the system, find where
the leverage actually is, then design the experiment. In that order.

That order is the whole game.

Which of the three — capability, opportunity, motivation — do you think blocks most change in
your world?

## Post 6 — Objection handling (AI + method vs. generic AI chat)

**Hook:** "Can't I just ask ChatGPT to do this?" Fair question. Here's my honest answer.

You can. A general AI chat will give you a smart-sounding answer about your change challenge in
thirty seconds. Sometimes that's enough.

Here's where it falls short for this specific job, and where CLEAR is built differently:

**It doesn't hold a method.** Ask a chat model and it improvises. CLEAR runs the same disciplined
sequence every time: clarify the behavior, map the system, locate leverage, design the
experiment. It does that because the order is what stops you from solving the wrong problem well.

**It doesn't show its uncertainty.** A chat answer sounds equally confident whether it knows or is
guessing. CLEAR separates what's grounded in your inputs from what it's assuming, and logs the
gaps.

**It isn't accountable.** With the Founding Partner Report, a licensed psychologist reads every
output and puts his name on it.

I'm not anti-AI. CLEAR *is* AI, pointed at one job and disciplined by a real methodology and a
real expert. That combination is the point.

## Spare A — Micro-lesson: the intention–action gap

**Hook:** The gap between what people intend to do and what they actually do is where most change
budgets go to die.

People genuinely intend to exercise, save more, adopt the new system, run the check-ins. Then
Monday comes and the intention loses to whatever is easier in the moment.

This is the intention–action gap, and it's one of the most reliable findings in behavioral
science. Intention is a weak predictor of behavior. It matters, but far less than we assume when
we design change around "getting buy-in."

What closes the gap isn't more conviction. It's usually one of three things:

- Making the behavior easier at the exact moment it needs to happen.
- Attaching it to a cue that already exists in the person's day.
- Removing a competing behavior that keeps winning.

Notice none of those is "motivate them harder."

Next time a change effort stalls despite everyone agreeing it's a good idea, don't add
inspiration. Look for the friction between intention and action, and remove it.

What's a small friction that quietly blocks a behavior on your team?

## Spare B — Micro-lesson: make the right behavior the easy behavior

**Hook:** If you want a behavior to happen, stop asking people to try harder. Make it the path of
least resistance.

We overrate willpower and underrate the environment. A person's behavior is shaped far more by
what's easy and what's in front of them than by how motivated they are on a given day.

Which means the highest-leverage move is often boring: reduce the number of steps, change the
default, put the right option where the hand already reaches.

[ERIK: insert a real, non-confidential example — a change that worked not by persuading people but
by making the desired action the easy one.]

This is why I distrust change plans built entirely on communication. Communication targets
motivation. But if the barrier is opportunity (friction, defaults, environment), no amount of
messaging fixes it. You have to change the path.

The question I ask on every engagement: if we did nothing to motivate anyone, and only made the
right behavior easier, how far would that get us?

Usually, further than the campaign would.

What's a default in your organization that's quietly working against the behavior you want?
